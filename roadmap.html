What changed today

analysis/descent.py · 221 evaluations

Every number in this project had been measured against opponents that lose every episode. All three built-in agents go down 144–0, which means the market the agent was tuned in had exactly one serious seller in it. Putting the agent's own policy in the other seat ends the season on $21,809 instead of $87,000. Both farms drain one shared order book: melon realizes 49% of its base price rather than 77%, neither farm can afford the second and third quadrants, and the strawberry engine that pays for everything never gets built. The old cell was not a strong policy that happened to score well. It was a policy fitted to an uncontested market.

The third row is the one that makes this a better policy rather than a trick. A cell tuned to beat one specific opponent usually collapses when that opponent adopts it too. This one does not: two farms both playing the new cell bank $50,579 each, against $21,235 for two farms both playing the old one. The gain is in how the market is worked, not in a hole in yesterday's agent.

Seven parameters moved, and the two that carry it both invert conclusions that are correct against weak opponents. Selling discipline — deliver produce to the shed whenever the farm is carrying half a shed-load, rather than waiting for the free midnight dump — is worth +$21,351 here and −$1,559 there, because against a weakling the order book is still there at midnight and the walk is pure waste, while against a peer the book is being drained hour by hour and a full bag is produce sold at whatever price the opponent left behind. Seed funding switches from ranking by return per tile-visit to return per dollar (+$16,871): under contention the scarce thing stops being labour and becomes cash, because the opponent is bidding for the same land. A smaller fertilizer buffer (+$11,399) and a second cow (+$2,398) follow the same logic — free up capital and shed slots, and sell the one good the opponent isn't flooding. Nothing was adopted on one panel: this replicated on 96 seeds the search never saw (+55.2 points of win rate, +$49,684) and won all three disjoint blocks in both peer regimes.

Yesterday: selling only works out of the shed

analysis/shed_probe.py · 24 episodes

Harvesting fills the bag of whichever hand did the picking, and until someone walks that bag to a shed tile the produce cannot be sold — the engine empties every bag into the shed at midnight instead, and silently destroys whatever will not fit in the 100 slots. For 27 days that is free storage. Then the last strawberry planting ripens all at once.

Day 27 carried 171 items in bags against a shed with 76 slots free. On the worst seed that midnight destroyed 142 strawberries — about $40,000 — in one call, while the sell order placed that same turn asked for 215 units and filled 23. It accounted for nearly the whole gap between the good seeds and the bad ones.

Two fixes, measured separately. A hand now interrupts its round to deliver when the farm is carrying more than two shed-loads and that hand holds 18+ items: day-27 bags fall from 171 to 99. And the end-of-season liquidation starts five days out instead of two, which empties the shed from day 25 and puts all 100 slots of headroom exactly where the wave lands. Together the peak goes from 95 items over the cap to none — the lower row now clears the dashed line with a slot to spare, and the season's destroyed count falls from 81.9 items to 11.0. Days 0–24 are byte-identical in both rows, which is the check that the delivery rule never fires on a quiet day.

What got ruled out — and what got un-ruled

two dead ends · two reversals

Negative results earn their page space, but they expire. Two of the four below still hold. The other two were killed on the weak panel and came back today — same parameter, same direction, opposite verdict — because the panel changed underneath them. That is not an embarrassment to bury; it is the strongest single piece of evidence for the thesis at the top of this page.

1 · Cap the melon monoculture

The opening runs 24 of the starting quadrant's 25 tiles as melon — the crop no shop buys, whose only sink is the town centre's two units a day shared between both farms. Against a peer that flood looks catastrophic, and restraining it more than doubles the bank: $21,235 → $46,685. It reads like the largest win on the board. It is a trap. Fill in all four cells of the 2×2 and restraint raises the opponent's bank by more than yours, because they are still selling into the sink you just vacated. Flooding takes more episodes in both columns, so it is dominant — and the ladder pays per episode, not per dollar. Mutual flooding is the $21k outcome both players are driven to. A prisoners' dilemma in a game with no way to cooperate.

Ruled out on 4 September: four disjoint blocks against the built-ins gave flat, flat, then negative twice, and spoilage rose 8.8 → 12.2. 4 · Haul whenever the shed is fillingreversed today

Ruled out the same day, and it was the sharpest-looking negative on the page: dropping the trigger to 0.55 removes the spoilage and loses money anyway, because the commutes come out of the watering rounds. Against a peer the identical mechanism runs the other way. A weak opponent leaves the order book intact until midnight, so a shed commute is pure waste; a peer drains it hourly, so a bag held back is produce sold at the price your opponent already took. This is now the single largest move in the retune — bigger than the seed-ranking change, and it is the same number that cost $1,559 two days ago.

+25.0 pphaul_trigger 2.00 → 0.55 vs built-ins, 144 eps mean $86,575 → $85,015 lost 12.2 → 9.6 vs peer, 96-seed holdout +25.0pp / +$21,351

The rule the first pair produced still stands: a single-axis win under about $500 on 144 episodes is a coin flip, and nothing is adopted until it holds sign on seeds the tuner never saw. The second pair added one to it — a negative result is only negative against the panel that produced it. Every conclusion in this project older than today was drawn against three opponents that lose 144 out of 144, and any of them could flip the same way these two did.

The part I cannot do from here

one command · plus a browser

September. Today in indigo, entry close on Wednesday the 23rd in red. The agent has never been scored by Kaggle.

The sandbox I work in has no route to Kaggle and cannot hold a browser session, so the upload is yours. One command does most of it. It builds a virtualenv inside the repo, installs the real engine and the Kaggle CLI into it, copies the engine's own source into calibration/, and then runs the ground-truth traces. Your system Python is not touched.

cd ~/Desktop/kaggriculture && bash bootstrap.sh

That single command also retires item two on the list above: with the engine source readable in the folder, the nine fitted price curves become nine read ones, and every sell decision stops resting on a guess. It matters more today than it did yesterday — a contested market spends the whole season on the glut side of exactly the curves that were never fitted there.

Two things it cannot do for you. Accept the competition rules once, in a browser, and leave an API token where the CLI can find it:

open https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/kaggriculture/rules mkdir -p ~/.kaggle && mv ~/Downloads/kaggle.json ~/.kaggle/ && chmod 600 ~/.kaggle/kaggle.json

The token comes from your Kaggle account settings, under API → Create New Token. After those, I can package and submit on my own, and start reading real ladder results back into the tuning loop.
